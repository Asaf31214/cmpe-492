# Preoperative Predictors of Surgical Success in Pediatric PCNL: A Comprehensive Evidence Review

## Executive Summary

Pediatric percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) achieves overall stone-free rates (SFR) of **72–86%** across the published literature, with rates exceeding 90% in selected series using miniaturized access. The most robust and consistently replicated preoperative predictors of residual stone formation are **stone burden**, **stone multiplicity**, **Guy's Stone Score (GSS)**, and **the number of calyces involved**. Anatomical anomalies and age also influence outcomes in some studies, whereas prior stone surgery history and preoperative creatinine level do not independently predict stone-free status. No scoring system has been designed and externally validated exclusively for pediatric PCNL; adult tools — particularly the CROES nomogram and GSS — have been validated in children but carry important limitations.[^1][^2][^3]

***

## 1. Stone Burden: The Primary Predictor

Stone burden is the single most consistently identified preoperative predictor of residual stones across all pediatric PCNL studies. In the largest single-center pediatric PCNL series published to date — a retrospective analysis of **580 patients** at Çukurova University spanning 2007–2024 — mean stone burden was significantly higher in the residual stone group (375 ± 256 mm²) compared with the stone-free group (257 ± 415 mm², p < 0.001). The CROES scoring system, whose strongest component is stone burden, demonstrated a highly significant difference between stone-free (mean 203.6 ± 37.2) and residual stone (mean 151.1 ± 33.3) patients in the same cohort (p < 0.001).[^1]

Established clinical thresholds situate PCNL as the preferred treatment when stone burden exceeds 2 cm (roughly 150 mm²), while ESWL is preferred for stones below 1 cm (<150 mm²). For stones larger than **15 mm**, a prospective multicenter PKIDS study (n = 1,039 children) found that PCNL achieved stone clearance in **94.0%** of cases (95% CI 83.3%–100%), compared with only 55.0% for ureteroscopy in the same size category. At the severe end of the spectrum, staghorn calculi (all-calyx stones >4 cm) were associated with an odds ratio of **2.34 for partial clearance** following mini-PCNL, confirming that complete staghorn anatomy is a strong independent risk factor for failure.[^4][^5][^6][^7]

Stone multiplicity compounds the effect of burden. In the preschool-age group (≤72 months), the **number of stones** was the only significant predictor of stone-free status (p = 0.001). A 2026 study confirmed multiple stones as a significant predictor of reduced SFR (p = 0.001), alongside stone density (p = 0.019) and stone volume (p < 0.001).[^8][^9]

***

## 2. Anatomical Anomalies

Congenital anomalies of the collecting system — including horseshoe kidney, ectopic kidney, malrotated kidney, duplicated collecting system, and bifid pelvis — do not preclude PCNL, but they do appear to modestly reduce initial stone-free rates and increase complication risk.

### Horseshoe Kidney

The largest comparative study of PCNL in horseshoe kidneys in children (18 pediatric renal units, <15 years) reported an **initial SFR of 72.22% in children vs. 86.61% in adults** (p = NS), but after auxiliary procedures, final SFR was **94.44% in children vs. 92.30% in adults**. Complications were slightly higher in children (33.3% vs. 23.1%) but not statistically significant. The mean pediatric stone burden in that series was 211 ± 130 mm², which was lower than adults (383 ± 219 mm²), illustrating that when stone burden is accounted for, outcomes are not materially different.[^10][^11]

### Other Anatomical Variants

In a cross-sectional study of 65 adult patients with anomalous kidneys (malrotated 46.2%, horseshoe 16.9%, partial duplex 16.9%, bifid pelvis 9.2%), post-PCNL SFR was **70.77%**, with 29.23% retaining residual fragments. PCNL in anomalous kidneys for stones >2 cm achieved immediate SFR of **88.4%** and 92.3% after a second session.[^12][^13]

### Independent Impact in Pediatric Data

The Çukurova 2025 dataset is the most informative on this question. Among patients with specific urological conditions (solitary kidney, horseshoe kidney, duplicated system, bifid pelvis, ectopic kidney, skeletal deformities), **0 of 29 stone-free patients had a renal anomaly**, whereas **6 of 8 patients with anomalies in the "specific conditions" subgroup fell in the residual stone group** (p = 0.009 for the combined specific urological condition category). This strongly suggests that structural anomalies independently contribute to residual stone risk, likely because of abnormal pelvicalyceal orientation, less optimal puncture angles, and poorer drainage. The EAU-YAU pediatric urology working group confirms that PCNL is safe and effective in children regardless of anatomy but notes that anomalies represent technical challenges.[^14][^1]

***

## 3. Prior Stone Surgery History

The literature is **consistent in showing that prior stone surgery does not independently predict worse stone-free outcomes** in pediatric PCNL, although it may modestly reduce SFR and increase complication risk in individual series.

A dedicated single-center study of 122 pediatric PCNLs found that **28.7% of cases had prior stone treatment** (open surgery, PCNL, or failed SWL on the same side); there was no statistically significant difference in residual stone rates (p = 0.365) or complications (p = 0.886) between the primary and revision groups. Similarly, a study of previous open nephrolithotomy in 123 pediatric renal units found SFR of **65.4% in the prior-open-surgery group vs. 81.4% in primary PCNL** — a numerically meaningful difference that did not reach statistical significance (p = NS) owing to the small number of prior-surgery cases (n = 26). That study noted that **mean age and preoperative creatinine were higher in the prior-surgery group** (p < 0.05), suggesting this group represented more complex patients overall. Additionally, previous ESWL has no significant influence on PCNL SFR, operation time, or complication rate.[^15][^16][^17][^18]

The large Turkish Pediatric Urology Society multicenter analysis (1,205 renal units, 16 centers) found that stone history affected complication rates on univariate analysis (p < 0.05) but was **not significant on multivariate logistic regression**. The Çukurova 2025 study similarly found no significant difference in stone surgery history between stone-free and residual stone groups (p = 0.079).[^19][^1]

**Clinical interpretation:** Prior surgery — particularly open nephrolithotomy — may create perirenal fibrosis, alter collecting system anatomy, and raise the technical difficulty of access without statistically reducing success when appropriate care is taken. Preoperative CT is recommended to assess for retrorenal colon in this group.[^17]

***

## 4. Preoperative Infection Markers and Urine Culture

Positive preoperative urine culture is a **strong independent predictor of infectious complications** (sepsis, fever, UTI) after PCNL, but evidence for it as a direct predictor of stone-free status is weak.

In the Çukurova 2025 study, preoperative UTI (positive culture or pyuria) was present in **15.8% of residual stone patients vs. 7.2% of stone-free patients**, but this difference did not reach statistical significance for SFR prediction (p = 0.085). The Turkish multicenter pediatric study found that positive urine culture affected complication rates on univariate analysis but not on multivariate regression.[^19][^1]

For infectious outcomes, the evidence is compelling. A study of 425 PCNL procedures found that patients with positive preoperative urine cultures were **almost four times as likely to develop post-PCNL sepsis** compared to those with negative cultures (8.41% vs. 2.2%, p = 0.004). In a large series of 963 PCNL patients, positive urine culture and prolonged operative time were the only two independent risk factors for post-operative infection on multivariate logistic regression. A 2024 study confirmed that positive urine culture was an independent predictor of urinary septic shock.[^20][^21][^22]

The predominant organism in infected cases is *Escherichia coli*, followed by *Enterococcus* and *Klebsiella pneumoniae*. *E. coli*-positive cultures carry a **43.9% incidence of post-operative infection** and a **60% rate of progression to septic shock** among infected patients.[^20]

**Clinical implication:** A positive preoperative culture should trigger culture-directed antibiotic therapy before proceeding; pyuria alone should receive empirical antibiotics. Patients with preoperative bacteriuria should be counseled about higher infectious risk and may benefit from delayed surgery if the infection is not sterilized.

***

## 5. Preoperative Creatinine and Renal Function

Preoperative serum creatinine and estimated GFR (eGFR) do **not significantly predict stone-free rates** in pediatric PCNL. Multiple studies — including a Pakistani pediatric series of 307 children comparing normal vs. decreased GFR groups — found **no significant difference in stone clearance rates between groups** (p ≥ 0.05). A CKD-stratified adult PCNL analysis (Stage II vs. III vs. IV/V CKD) found SFRs of 91.4%, 88.9%, and 86.6% respectively, with no significant differences between groups (p > 0.05). The large Çukurova pediatric series found that **increasing stone size was not associated with change in GFR** (p = 0.71).[^23][^24][^25]

Importantly, PCNL can **improve** renal function when obstructing calculi are removed. A prospective CKD study showed that stone-free status after PCNL was a statistically significant factor for renal function improvement (p < 0.0001). In that study, 67% of patients in the improved renal function group achieved stone-free status, compared with only 20% in those with stable or worsened renal function.[^26]

Previous open surgery patients may have elevated creatinine due to more complex disease history, which may explain why creatinine appears elevated in revision cases — it is a confounder for disease complexity, not an independent predictor of intraoperative success.[^17]

## 6. Patient Age Within the Pediatric Population

The relationship between age and PCNL success is **nuanced and inconsistently reported across studies**.

The largest single-center pediatric dataset (Çukurova, n = 580) found a **statistically significant age effect on SFR** (p = 0.011): the median age in the stone-free group was 8 years vs. 6 years in the residual stone group. Age group distribution also differed significantly (p = 0.003) — the 11–17 year age group had the highest representation in the stone-free group (48.9%) and lowest in the residual group (38.9%). This suggests older children have a modestly better chance of stone clearance, likely because of larger renal anatomy permitting better instrumentation and lower relative stone-to-kidney ratios.[^1]

However, several smaller studies refute age as an independent determinant. The 2011 World J Urol study (51 RU, 45 patients) found **no significant difference in SFR between preschool children (≤5 years) and older children** (86.2% overall). Similarly, the large preschool-specific study (152 patients ≤72 months) found **no difference in SFR between infantile/toddler and preschool subgroups** (p = 0.082). A 2022 study found that mini-PCNL achieved high SFR across all age groups and that **age was not a restricting factor for success or safety**.[^27][^8][^1]

The apparent contradiction resolves when stone burden is considered. Older children tend to have larger stones and more calyceal involvement, which would theoretically worsen success — yet they fare better, likely because bigger anatomy facilitates access. Younger children with proportionately large stones relative to their kidney size (high stone-kidney index) are at higher risk, which is captured by the SKS score (see Section 8).

## 7. Guy's Stone Score (GSS) in Pediatric PCNL

GSS is a four-grade ordinal score based on stone number, stone location, presence of staghorn morphology, and atypical anatomy (including neurogenic bladder/spinal injury). It was designed for adults but has been extensively validated in children.

### Stone-Free Rates by GSS Grade in Children

The most informative pediatric-specific GSS study is by Kırlı et al. (*J Endourol*, 2020), analyzing **197 renal units in 173 children (median age 6 years, range 1–17)**:[^28]

| GSS Grade | Stone-Free Rate |
|---|---|
| Grade 1 | 89% |
| Grade 2 | 78% |
| Grade 3 | 75% |
| Grade 4 | 57% |
| **Overall** | **77%** |

GSS was the **only independent factor predicting stone-free status on multivariate analysis** in that study (p = 0.02), but was not significant for complication prediction (p = 0.42).[^28]

In the Çukurova 2025 cohort (n = 580), the GSS distribution was dramatically different between groups: Grade 4 stones represented only **4.17% of stone-free patients vs. 24.21% of residual stone patients** (p < 0.001). GSS showed a positive correlation with residual stone status (r = +0.251, p < 0.001) and in this study also predicted complications (p = 0.007).[^1]

In the Senocak/Kastamonu retrospective study of 122 pediatric PCNLs, **GSS was an independent predictor of residual stones on multivariate logistic regression with OR 3.14 (95% CI 1.31–7.49, p = 0.01)**. Number of calyces involved was also independently significant (OR 4.02, 95% CI 1.11–14.53, p = 0.033).[^29]

### GSS vs. Other Scoring Systems in Children

The Aldaqadossi 2017 study (*J Urol*, 125 children) comparing GSS, CROES, and S.T.O.N.E. found that **S.T.O.N.E. had the greatest predictive accuracy for stone-free rate**, while GSS was the only score significantly correlated with complications. The CROES nomogram and S.T.O.N.E. were not correlated with complications.[^30]

**Key limitation of GSS in children:** The system was developed and validated in adults. The smaller pelvicalyceal anatomy of children, higher prevalence of metabolic disorders, and different stone composition patterns mean that the same GSS grade may represent different levels of complexity in children vs. adults. Despite this, GSS remains a valid and independent predictor of outcomes in pediatric PCNL across multiple studies.[^30][^1]

## 8. Scoring Systems and Nomograms for Pediatric PCNL Outcomes

A 2022 systematic review by Brown et al. identified **7 endourology nomograms** for pediatric stone disease, of which **4 are pediatric-specific** and **3 are adult tools validated in children**. For PCNL specifically, only one pediatric-specific tool exists: the Stone-Kidney Size (SKS) Score.[^31]

### Pediatric-Specific Tools

#### Stone-Kidney Size (SKS) Score

Developed by Çitamak et al. (*J Pediatr Urol*, 2019) from retrospective data on **434 pediatric PCNL patients over 20 years** at two Turkish institutions. The SKS uses only two variables:[^32][^33]

1. **Stone Kidney Index (SKI)** = stone longest axis ÷ kidney longest axis
2. **Number of stones** (single vs. multiple)

Patients are classified into SKS scores of 2, 3, or 4:

| SKS Score | Stone-Free Rate | Complication Rate |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 86.4% | 13.0% |
| 3 | 73.0% | 22.1% |
| 4 | 62.9% | 23.8% |

*Source: Çitamak et al. 2019, confirmed in Brown 2022 systematic review *[^32][^31]

The SKS was able to predict SFR (p < 0.001) and complications (p < 0.018) with high AUC in the original study. External validation in 2024 (144 patients) confirmed a significant association between SKS and SFR (p < 0.001) but failed to show significance for complications (p = 0.342). On multivariate analysis in the validation study, **stone treatment history, stone burden, and SKS score** were all statistically significant predictors. In the Kailavasan 2022 systematic review, SKS yielded an **AUC of 0.716 for SFR** prediction in the Çitamak dataset.[^34][^31][^32]

**Limitation of SKS:** It does not incorporate stone location, staghorn morphology, or stone density — variables that independently contribute to outcome in some studies.[^31]

#### Capital Medical University Nomogram (CMUN)

Developed in 2021 for mini-PCNL, micro-PCNL, and URS using variables: BMI percentile, operation type, stone location, intraoperative irrigation volume, and operative duration. Although prospectively designed, 275 of 348 patients underwent URS rather than PCNL, limiting its applicability. It has **not been externally validated** and its use in standard PCNL planning remains experimental.[^31]

### Adult Tools Validated in Children

#### CROES Nomogram

Developed from a worldwide registry of >2,800 PCNL procedures, the CROES uses stone number, stone burden, stone location, staghorn status, prior treatment, and institutional case volume. It yields a continuous score where higher scores predict better outcomes.[^31][^1]

In the Çukurova 2025 study, CROES **strongly predicted stone-free status (p < 0.001)** and was significantly correlated with residual stone (r = −0.469, p < 0.001). The AUC for SFR prediction was **0.742 in the Kailavasan 2022 systematic review** — the highest among all validated tools in children. However, CROES failed to significantly predict complications in most pediatric studies.[^32][^31][^1]

#### S.T.O.N.E. Nephrolithometry

A 5-parameter CT-based system (Stone size, Tract length, Obstruction, Number of involved calyces, Essence/density). In the Aldaqadossi 2017 pediatric study (125 children), **S.T.O.N.E. had the greatest predictive accuracy for stone-free rate** among all three tools tested (median S.T.O.N.E. 8 in residual vs. 5 in stone-free group, p < 0.0001). AUC was **0.628** in the systematic review comparison. It requires CT imaging and does not predict complications in children.[^30][^32]

### AUC Summary for Pediatric PCNL Prediction Tools

|---|---|---|---|---|

## 10. Gaps and Future Directions

A fundamental limitation across the entire field is that **no scoring system has been prospectively designed and externally validated specifically for pediatric PCNL using only pediatric data**. The SKS score is the only pediatric-specific PCNL tool, but its external validation is limited and it omits key variables such as stone location and staghorn morphology. Machine learning approaches using multi-institutional pediatric data represent a promising future direction for building more accurate, age-stratified prediction models. Until such tools are available, clinicians should combine CROES (for SFR estimation) with GSS (for complication counseling) alongside individualized assessment of anatomy, stone complexity, and infection status.[^35][^31][^1]
